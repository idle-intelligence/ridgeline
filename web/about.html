about ridgeline

ridgeline draws eleven real worlds and the Sun: Earth, the Moon, Mars, Venus, Mercury, Ceres, Vesta, Enceladus, Pluto, Charon. Each one is a globe of stacked Joy Division "Unknown Pleasures" ridgelines. Every ridge is real data. It runs entirely in your browser.

The image behind it

The stacked-ridgeline look is the cover of Joy Division's 1979 album Unknown Pleasures, designed by Peter Saville. That cover is a real scientific plot: about a hundred stacked radio pulses from the pulsar PSR B1919+21 (now also known as CP 1919), the first pulsar ever discovered, by Jocelyn Bell Burnell and Antony Hewish in 1967.

The stacked-pulse figure was produced by Harold D. Craft Jr. for his 1970 Cornell PhD thesis (from Arecibo data), and reached print through the Cambridge Encyclopaedia of Astronomy (1977) and Scientific American, where Saville found it. Scientific American later tracked down the original figure and interviewed Craft about it.

ridgeline wraps that same stacked-profile idea around planets: each latitude ring is an elevation profile sweeping longitude.

The terrain is real

Every ridge is real elevation, or for the Sun real magnetic field. All sources are public-domain government data.

The Sun's height isn't height

The Sun has no surface to measure. Its ridges are its magnetic field: a HMI synoptic magnetogram covering one full solar rotation (Carrington rotation 2300), where the signed field strength in gauss is mapped to elevation (a signed square-root curve, capped so active-region spikes read as mountains rather than needles).

Bright ridges are magnetic active regions: the sunspot belts. Because a synoptic map is stitched strip by strip as the Sun turns, longitude is also time: flying across the map crosses about 27 days of observation.

Views & units

Every world is drawn at the same size. Whatever you are orbiting, the Sun or Enceladus, its sea-level sphere has a radius of 6000 world units, the internal unit everything is measured in. A body's true radius never changes how big it looks; it only sets the conversion the HUD uses to report your altitude in kilometres:

altitude km = altitude wu × (body radius km ÷ 6000)

So one world unit is 1.06 km at Earth, 0.04 km at Vesta, and 116 km at the Sun. The same flight feels the same everywhere but reads very differently on the dial.

The altitude bands work the same way. The ceilings are fixed in world units, identical for every body, so each band spans a wildly different real distance depending on where you are:

  • SURFACEbelow 50 wu · 53 km at Earth, 2 km at Vesta
  • ATMO / LOW / CORONAbelow 1500 wu · 1,593 km at Earth, 66 km at Vesta, 174,000 km at the Sun
  • ORBITbelow 12,000 wu · 12,742 km at Earth
  • DEEP SPACEbeyond that

The second band is named for what you are actually in: ATMO on the three worlds with an atmosphere (Earth, Mars, Venus), LOW on the airless and icy ones, CORONA at the Sun. Past deep space the globe shrinks and cross-fades into the solar-system view, starting at 120,000 wu and complete by 320,000 wu.

Why the mountains are too big

At true scale this would not be much of a picture: Everest is 8.8 km on a 6371 km planet, about 8 world units of bulge on a 6000-unit sphere. Invisible. So relief is exaggerated, and by how much depends on how close you are. Near the ground the exaggeration is ×2.75; climbing away it eases up to ×14, which keeps continents readable from orbit without turning low passes into spikes.

On top of that each body carries its own factor, tuned so its signature terrain reads right. Mars is damped to 0.45, because Olympus Mons is the tallest thing in the solar system and at full exaggeration it swallows the globe. Pluto is pushed to 1.4 because the New Horizons terrain is the whole point of visiting. The Sun sits at 1.5.

Four bodies opt out entirely. Ceres, Vesta, Enceladus and Charon are small enough that their real relief is already a visible fraction of their radius (±1% for Enceladus, ±2.3% for Charon), so they are rendered at a fixed exaggeration that preserves the true ratio. Vesta genuinely is that lumpy; its axes differ by 60 km.

The solar system is squeezed

Drawn honestly, an orrery is mostly empty: put Pluto on screen and the inner planets collapse into the Sun. So only the radial distance is compressed, logarithmically:

display radius = log₁₀(1 + AU) ÷ log₁₀(1 + 40)

Zero at the Sun, 1.0 at 40 AU, roughly Pluto's orbit. Everything else is real. Each body's direction is its true heliocentric position from a Kepler ephemeris, so the ecliptic longitudes, the spacing order, and the tilt of Pluto's 17°-inclined orbit are all correct. Only the ruler is bent.

How it's built

  • Everything runs in your browser. No server, no build step, no framework. Vanilla JavaScript static files.
  • The terrain is baked offline in Python into compact int16 heightfields. They live in a Hugging Face dataset, are fetched once and kept in your browser's Cache API, and stream coarse-to-fine, so the globe appears in about a second and then sharpens. A small WebAssembly module holds the heightfield in memory for the GPU to read.
  • Every frame, WebGPU compute shaders map the grid onto the sphere, cull it against the horizon, pick the level of detail and emit the ridgelines on the GPU. The lines you see are computed, never stored.
  • The camera, the Kepler ephemeris and the orrery are plain JavaScript. The ephemeris is approximate in absolute terms, but the relative geometry and motion are real.
  • This needs a browser with WebGPU. There is no fallback renderer; without it the page says so and stops.
